Evaluation methodology · 30 minutes

LLM-as-a-Judge Is Probably Lying to You

Your judge is a measuring instrument. Nobody calibrated it, nobody reports its error bars, and it goes quiet exactly when the answer is hard. Here is how to catch it — and what to publish instead.

Su Myat NoeNII / LLMC, Tokyo github.com/ImSuMyatNoe/judge-audit
0:00 – 1:00 Open cold. "Everyone in this room has shipped a number that came out of an LLM judge. I have. This talk is about the four things that number does not tell you, and the seven-line checklist that fixes it."
Do not apologise for the title. Say up front: judges are not useless — unaudited judges are. Everything in the live demo runs offline; the repo link is on this slide and the last slide.

The number that convinced us

“GPT-4 agrees with humans 85% of the time.”

It is a real result, from a good paper, and it is the sentence that put a judge in your pipeline.

It is also 85% of non-tie pairs. Count the ties and the same paper reports 66%.

The most cited summarization result for a GPT-4 judge is Spearman 0.514 with humans. Not 0.9.

What we actually bought

Cheap, fast, reproducible-looking, and it scales past what any annotation budget can reach. Those are real wins. I am not here to take them away.

But we bought a measuring instrument and never asked for its datasheet: precision, drift, failure modes, dead zones.

Zheng et al. 2023, Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena, NeurIPS D&B (arXiv:2306.05685) · Liu et al. 2023, G-Eval, EMNLP (arXiv:2303.16634)

1:00 – 3:00 The point is not that Zheng et al. were wrong — they were careful and the caveat is in their own table. The point is what survived the telephone game into your team's eval doc. Ask the room: who has an 85% or an r=0.8 in a slide somewhere? Hands go up. Good.

The frame for the next 25 minutes

A judge is an instrument.
Treat it like one.

No lab would accept a thermometer with no repeatability spec, no documented dead zone, and no calibration date. We accept exactly that from a judge, because it answers in fluent English and the answer has a decimal point in it.

Four lies, in order of how much they cost you

  • “I am a function.” Same input, different score.
  • “I answered.” It went quiet on the hard ones.
  • “We agree 75% of the time.” Before chance.
  • “I read the answer.” It read the formatting.

Then: a live demo you can re-run tonight, and the checklist.

3:00 – 4:00 Set expectations: four lies, one demo, one checklist. Say that the demo data is synthetic and labelled as such — you will say it again when the terminal is up, because someone always asks, and you want to be the one who raised it.

Lie one

“I am a function of the input.”

21%of items scored the same on all three passes
27%changed pass/fail verdict between passes
If you ran it once, a quarter of your labels are a coin flip you did not know you were flipping.

Demo figures, run live in Act 1 · Haldar et al. 2025, EMNLP (arXiv:2510.27106): identical-verdict-on-all-three-runs for one judge, 61.3% · Lau et al. 2026 (arXiv:2603.04417): score variability at temperature 0

4:00 – 6:30 The single most persuasive slide in the deck. Let the picture sit for a beat before you say a number. "Same input. Same model. Same temperature. Three passes."
If someone says "set temperature to 0": that is the next slide — and Tamba 2026 shows even pinned temperature leaves some borderline items non-reproducible, while newer judge models have removed the temperature control altogether.

Lie one · what to do

Temperature 0 is necessary and not sufficient

If you set nothing

Providers default to temperature 1.0. Up to ~50% per-item pass/fail disagreement across 20 identical runs.

If you pin it

Better — and still not deterministic. Borderline items stay unstable, and some recent judge models removed the temperature control.

What survives

Run three passes and report the spread. Stability is something you measure, not something you configure.

Report the score and the spread. Add ICC(1) — the share of score variance that comes from real differences between items rather than from the judge's own noise. In the demo: 0.74.

Tamba 2026 (arXiv:2606.26185) · Norman et al. 2026 (arXiv:2606.19544): two production judges show test-retest reliability >0.95 and position bias >0.10 — stable is not the same as unbiased

6:30 – 8:00 Pre-empt the room's favourite objection and then give them the positive move: three passes, report spread, report ICC. Note that "reliable" and "valid" are different words — a judge can be perfectly repeatable and perfectly biased.

Lie two

“I answered your question.”

Blank. Unparseable. Refused. Filtered. Timed out. Your harness has a try / except: continue in it, and that line is deleting your most important data.

Missing is not random. It is a map of where the judge could not tell.

The items that come back empty are the contested ones — the occluded sign, the hedged answer, the case that needed a judgement call. Exactly the items you built the eval to cover.

And when you retry them, the judge does not say “I cannot tell”. It returns a safe middling score.

8:00 – 10:00 Personal hook here: describe finding blank cells in your own judge output and assuming it was a parsing bug. It was not — rerunning the blanks returned valid scores, so it was the API being nondeterministic, and the blanks clustered on the weakest models' most ambiguous outputs. That is when this talk started.

Lie two · the consequence

Your correlation lives on the easy items

On the quarter of items where the answer was never in doubt, the judge agrees with you. On the quarter that needed judgement, it is guessing — and that is the quarter it also refused to score 57% of the time.

A pooled correlation averages those two populations and reports a number that describes neither.
10:00 – 11:30 This is the analytical heart of the talk. Say it plainly: your judge is confirming the cases you did not need a judge for.
Stratify by anything you have: annotator confidence, occlusion, item length, source dataset, which model produced the response.

Lie two · from my own runs

The honest number is the worse number

In a Japanese multimodal-safety evaluation I ran this year, one hosted judge returned blanks on a slice of items. I added a retry-on-blank loop, up to three attempts, and recovered almost all of them.

0.73judge–human correlation, blanks dropped
~0.5after the blanks were recovered

What that drop is not

It is not the judge getting worse. Nothing about the judge changed.

What it is

The dataset finally including the hard, low-agreement cases the judge had been silently excluding. The 0.73 was a survivorship artefact. The lower number is the real estimate, and it is the one that belongs in the paper.

Publish the correlation after recovery, and say how many items were recovered. A reviewer who sees only the first number is being misled, even if nobody meant to.
11:30 – 13:00 Own this one in the first person — it is your result and it is the most credible moment in the talk. Emphasise that you nearly reported the 0.73, because it was the number that came out of the clean run.
If asked which judge: say it is not the point, the mechanism is general, and the same nondeterminism is documented across providers.

Lie three

“We agree 75% of the time.”

Most eval items are easy and both raters say “fine” a lot. Raw agreement prices that in as skill.

A judge that says pass to literally everything scores 80% agreement on a set that is 80% passes, and knows nothing. Its κ is exactly zero.

Chance-correcting deflates raw agreement by 33–41 points of κ on MT-Bench across 21 judges and ~541,000 judgments.

Norman et al. 2026 (arXiv:2606.19544) · Gao et al. 2025, NAACL (arXiv:2410.16834): which correlation measure you pick changes the meta-evaluation conclusion

13:00 – 14:30 Keep this act short and mechanical — it is the least controversial of the four and the room will accept it quickly. The memorable line is the always-says-pass judge. Practical ask: never ship a raw agreement percentage without the majority baseline next to it.

The JudgeBench row is the one that lands with engineers: on questions with a checkable right answer, the judge is at chance, while looking great on the benchmark you chose it with.

Lie four · the case that matters

Confident, fluent, and wrong

A graded 0–10 judge answers one question: is this a good answer? It never answers the other one: does this mean what the reference means?

In a legally grounded task those come apart exactly where it hurts. A response that confidently misreads a sign — fluent, well-formatted, decisive — scores well on style and fails on law.

Pair the graded judge with a strict binary semantic-equivalence judge against a human-curated reference, and require both to pass.

Dual-judge protocol: Noe, Nguyen, Zin & Satoh 2026, Beyond Accuracy: A Dual-Judge Evaluation Protocol for Vision-Language Models in Legally Grounded Tasks · github.com/ImSuMyatNoe/dual-judge-traffic-signs

16:30 – 18:00 This is your contribution — say so, briefly, and keep it about the mechanism rather than the paper. The design is cheap: one extra binary call, and the two judges answer different questions rather than voting on the same one.
Key honest detail: the false-reject rate does not get worse. That is what makes it a free lunch, and it is the first thing a careful listener will check.

Worse in multimodal

When the judge cannot see, it still answers

Modality neglect

Across 26 multimodal judges and 9 bias types: judges systematically ignore the image or the text when evidence is missing or conflicting.

Lee et al. 2026, ACL Main (arXiv:2604.18164)

Pointwise is worse than pairwise

Multimodal judges look human-like on pairwise comparison and diverge sharply on pointwise scoring — with hallucinated justifications.

Chen et al. 2024, ICML oral (arXiv:2402.04788)

And the reference moves

A traffic sign means what the local statute says it means. “Plausible reading” and “legally correct reading” are different targets, and only one of them is what you are evaluating.

If your judge grades text about an image, verify that it used the image. The cheapest probe: re-run a sample with the image removed and see how much the score moves.
18:00 – 19:00 Short bridge slide, grounded in your own domain (traffic-sign interpretation for legally grounded AI). The image-ablation probe is a concrete, memorable thing anyone can run this week.

The number your team can act on

“r = 0.7” is not a decision

74%correct calls at 100 items per system
400items needed for 90% correct calls
Nobody can act on a correlation. Everybody can act on “at our eval size, this judge names the wrong winner one time in four”.

And a hard ceiling: when the judge is no more accurate than the model under test, no debiasing method can cut the ground-truth labels you need by more than half — Dorner & Hardt 2025, ICLR (arXiv:2410.13341)

25:00 – 26:30 This is the slide that changes behaviour in an industry room, because it is the only one phrased as a shipping decision. Convert judge noise into P(wrong call) at their eval size.
The Dorner & Hardt line is the one to leave them with if there is time: a weak judge cannot rank models better than itself, no matter how you post-process it.

The checklist · before you trust it

Six things, none of them expensive

  • Run it three times on the same inputs. Report the score and the spread.
  • Count the blanks, then retry them. Report the correlation after recovery — the worse, honest one.
  • Stratify by difficulty before you believe any correlation.
  • Chance-correct every agreement number, with the majority baseline beside it.
  • Swap the order and re-judge every pairwise comparison. One extra call per pair.
  • Regress score on length with quality controlled for. If length wins, your leaderboard ranks verbosity.
26:30 – 27:30 Go fast — one line each, no elaboration. They have the repo. The whole checklist is in docs/checklist.md as one printable page.

The checklist · before you let it decide

Three more, and what to publish

  • Pair the graded judge with a binary check against a human reference. Require both.
  • Convert noise into decision risk at your eval size, and report that.
  • Pin and log everything — model version, temperature, score range, prompt, parser — and re-audit when the vendor ships.

In the paper or the eval report

Number of judge passes and the spread · blank rate and how blanks were handled · correlation before and after recovery · agreement chance-corrected, with the baseline · judge version, temperature, score range, prompt, parser · whether both orders were judged · eval-set size and the decision risk at that size.

A pinned model name is not a pinned model: same endpoint, same prompts, GPT-4's accuracy on one task fell from 84% to 51% over three months in 2023.

Chen, Zaharia & Zou 2023 (arXiv:2307.09009)

Close

Judges are not useless.
Unaudited judges are.

None of this makes the judge honest. It makes the judge's dishonesty measurable — which is the most you can ask of any instrument, and exactly what we ask of every other one we publish with.
