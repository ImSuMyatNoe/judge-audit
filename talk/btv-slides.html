Plain english  //  for everyone in the room

What an LLM judge
actually is

The idea

You cannot pay humans to read a million model outputs. So you ask another model: “here is a task, here is what the agent did — score it 0 to 10.”

Why it caught on

It is cheap, instant, scales past any annotation budget, and it agrees with human raters often enough to look trustworthy.

What it quietly became

A measuring instrument — and one nobody asked for a datasheet: no repeatability spec, no dead zones, no calibration date.

You would not accept a kitchen scale with no repeatability spec. We accept it from a judge because the answer arrives in fluent English with a decimal point in it.
5:30 – 6:45 The slide that keeps the non-researchers with you. Do not skip it even if the room looks technical — the kitchen-scale line is what everyone quotes back afterwards.
"Instrument" is the metaphor the whole talk runs on. Plant it here.

Why agents  //  make all of this worse

One agent run is
not one judgement

A single-turn answer gets one judgement. An agent trajectory gets one per step — and the errors do not cancel, they compound.

Hold that thought. In four minutes we will put a real number on how fast it compounds, and it is worse than anyone guesses.
6:45 – 8:00 This slide is why the talk is about agents and not just chatbots — and it sets up the compounding arithmetic on slide 6, which is the single most surprising number you have.
Do not reveal it yet. Promise it.

Clue 01  //  what it means for an agent

A 27% flip rate
does not stay at 27%

7.7%of 8-step trajectories judged the same way twice
Re-run your agent eval tomorrow and most trajectories change verdict — without a single line of the agent changing.

Assumes steps are judged independently, which is the common setup. Correlated steps soften it; they do not remove it.

10:30 – 12:00 The number that makes the room go quiet. Say the arithmetic out loud: 73% for one step, and eight steps of that is 8%.
Be honest about the independence assumption in the same breath — it costs you nothing and it is the first thing a careful listener will test.

Clue 02  //  what it means

Your agreement lives
on the easy items

The judge agrees with you on the cases you never needed a judge for. On the contested quarter it is guessing — and that is also the quarter where it returned nothing more than half the time.

A single pooled correlation averages two different populations and describes neither.

Correlation here just means: when humans rate something highly, does the judge too? 1.0 is perfect agreement, 0 is a coin flip.

14:00 – 15:15 The plain-English gloss on correlation is there for the mixed room — read it out, do not assume.
Practical ask: stratify by anything you have — task type, trajectory length, tool count, annotator confidence.

Clue 03  //  agreement that isn't

“It agrees with humans
75% of the time”

Most eval items are easy, and both raters say “fine” a lot. Raw agreement counts all of that as skill.

A judge that says pass to everything scores 80% agreement on a set that is 80% passes — and knows nothing.

Kappa is agreement after you subtract the credit for lucky guesses. Same data, honest scale.

Chance-correcting deflates raw agreement by 33–41 points of kappa across 21 judges and ~541,000 judgments — Norman et al. 2026 (arXiv:2606.19544)

15:15 – 16:30 Shortest clue. The always-says-pass judge is the whole argument; everything else is bookkeeping.
Practical ask: never ship a raw agreement percentage without the baseline next to it.

The reveal  //  who did it

The judge isn't broken.
It was never calibrated.

What we assumed

A judge is a cheaper human rater. If it agrees with humans often enough, the number it produces means what a human's number would mean.

What it actually is

An instrument with noise, dead zones and drift — none of which are reported, because we never asked. Every clue tonight was a missing spec on a datasheet nobody wrote.

Not one of these four clues required the agent under test to be bad. The suspect was the measurement the whole time.
18:30 – 19:30 The turn of the detective story. Slow down. This is where the audience gets the satisfaction of the reveal, and it is the line they will repeat to their team.

Fix 01  //  for agent evaluation specifically

Deterministic first.
Judge last.

Question about a trajectoryWho should answer it
Did it call the tools in the required order?Assertion
Did the arguments match the schema?Assertion
Did it refuse the ineligible refund?Assertion
Did it recover from the 503?Assertion
Was the final reply actually helpful?Judge
Was the tone right for an upset customer?Judge

Most of what we ask a judge about an agent is not a matter of taste. It is a fact about the trace, and a plain assertion answers it perfectly, for free, every time.

Use the judge only where a deterministic check genuinely cannot reach — and then audit it, because that is exactly where you cannot see it fail.
19:30 – 21:00 This slide complements the other talk in the session rather than repeating it: deterministic invariants are the right tool for most agent questions, and the judge is a last resort.
Practical framing: every row you move from "judge" to "assertion" is a row that stops being noisy and starts being free.

Fix 02  //  the number your team can act on

“r = 0.7” is not a decision

74%correct calls at 100 items per system
400items needed to reach 90%
Nobody can act on a correlation. Everybody can act on “at our eval size, this judge names the wrong winner one time in four”.
21:00 – 22:30 The slide that changes behaviour in an industry room, because it is phrased as a shipping decision rather than a statistic. Convert judge noise into P(wrong call) at their eval size.

Fix 03  //  clue by clue

What to do about
each one

ClueWhat goes wrongWhat fixes it
01 One pass, and the score is partly a coin flip Run three passes. Report the median and the spread.
02 Unparseable rows dropped, inflating agreement Retry blanks and keep them. Publish the recovered number.
03 Raw agreement counts lucky guesses as skill Chance-correct, and print the baseline beside it.
04 A graded score rewards style over correctness Add a binary check against a reference; require both.
Every fix is one more API call and one more column in a CSV. None of them needs a new model, a fine-tune, or an annotation budget.
22:30 – 24:00 Walk the right-hand column only — they have already seen the middle one demonstrated. Land the closing line about cost: this is the slide that makes the whole thing feel doable on Monday.

Take home  //  the checklist

Seven things,
none expensive

  • Run the judge three times on the same inputs, and report the spread.
  • Count the blanks, then retry them — and publish the number you get afterwards.
  • Stratify by difficulty before you believe any single correlation.
  • Chance-correct every agreement figure, with the baseline printed beside it.
  • Pair the graded judge with a binary check against a human reference.
  • Move every checkable question to an assertion and leave the judge only what needs taste.
  • Report decision risk, not correlation — and re-audit when the vendor ships a new version.
24:00 – 26:00 One line each, fast. Tell them it is one page in the repo so nobody photographs it in a panic.

Take home  //  when to trust it at all

Automate, or ask
a human?

Measured reliability means the numbers from tonight — spread across passes and chance-corrected agreement — not a vendor benchmark and not a figure from a blog post.

Cost of a wrong accept is yours to know. A tone check and a refund approval are not in the same quadrant, even behind the same API.

The quadrant nobody staffs is top right: high stakes, and the judge looks reliable. That is where a single number is most expensive.
26:00 – 28:00 The most photographed slide of the talk. Be explicit that the framework is only as good as the measurement — the first twenty minutes exist to put a defensible number on the horizontal axis.

Close

Judges aren't useless.
Unaudited judges are.

None of this makes the judge honest. It makes the judge's dishonesty measurable — which is all we ask of any other instrument we publish with.
