Before we start

Why this one matters to you

If you are building with AI right now, something in your stack is already being graded by a model. A pull request, a support reply, a summary, a refund.

Nobody sat down and voted on that. It just happened, quietly, over about eighteen months, because it was the only thing that could keep up.

And that grade is what decides which version ships, which prompt wins, and which agent goes live on Monday.

why care This is the contract with the room. Say it slowly and look at people. The point is not that anybody was careless. The point is that nobody checked.

A few words first  //  one of four

🤖 Agent

A model that takes actions on its own. Not just answering your question, but running through several steps until something is actually done.

  • It takes real actionsIt calls tools, looks up your order, sends a refund, or passes the case to a human.
  • It thinks and adaptsIt mixes your fixed business rules with its own reasoning, and picks a path as it goes.
  • It needs a new kind of testingYou have to check the path it took, not just the sentence it ended up writing.
  • It fails in new waysWrong tool, endless loops, and made up arguments. Those three come up again and again.
terminology, one of four Half this room builds these for a living and half have never written a prompt. Say it once, plainly, and do not apologise for defining it. "Actions" is the word to land.

A few words first  //  two of four

⚖️ LLM as a judge

A second model that reads the first one's work and gives it a score, so nobody has to sit and read two thousand refund letters by hand.

  • What you feed itThe photo, the order, your policy, and the reply the agent wrote. Sometimes the whole trace.
  • What it hands backA number out of ten, plus a paragraph explaining the number.
  • Why everyone uses itIt keeps up with the volume, it costs cents, and it gives you something you can put in a chart.
  • The catchThat explanation paragraph is written to convince you, and nothing anywhere checks whether it's true.
terminology, two of four "One model does the work, another marks the homework." Point at the catch and say it is the whole reason you are here tonight.

A few words first  //  three of four

📊 Eval

The test you run on your AI to decide whether it's good enough to ship, and the only thing standing between a bug and your customers.

  • A benchmarkA fixed set of cases everybody tests on, so two numbers can be compared.
  • A pass rateThe share of those cases your system got right. That is the number that ends up on the slide.
  • A gateThe line you've decided not to ship below, and the reason anybody cares about the number.
  • Why it matters tonightEvery figure in this talk came out of an eval that trusted a judge to do the scoring.
terminology, three of four Everyone in the room uses this word and nobody defines it the same way. Say the gate line slowly, because that is the one that makes eval work feel worth funding.

A few words first  //  four of four

🧾 Trace and ground truth

Two words the rest of the talk leans on, so let's get them out of the way now.

  • TraceThe full record of what the agent did, step by step, in the order it did it.
  • Ground truthWhat a careful human says the right answer actually was. Slow, expensive, and worth it.
  • Why you want bothThe trace shows you where it went wrong. Ground truth is how you know it went wrong at all.
  • The gapA judge with no ground truth behind it is a second opinion, not a measurement.
terminology, four of four Keep this one quick. It exists so that clue two and clue four do not need explaining mid flow.

Twenty minutes later

It comes, and it is not your pizza.

So you take a photo of the bag, you say the order is wrong, and you ask for your money back. Nothing unusual about any of that.

What is unusual is that nobody will ever open that photo. Nine times out of ten a model reads it first, and it has about four seconds to make up its mind.

That is already how most refund messages get answered here. The open question was never whether to automate it. The open question is who checks the answers.

the case Say the amount out loud once so it feels like money. Twenty two dollars. Everything later in the talk is this one order.

Three ways this can go

Somebody has to call it.

💰refund you ❌reject the claim 👤pass it to a human

Refund a claim that was never true and the company quietly bleeds. Reject a real one and that customer is gone for good. Send everything to a person and there was no point automating anything in the first place.

Making the call is the cheap part. Knowing whether the call was any good is the expensive part, and that is the whole talk.

stakes Keep it light. The point is that all three answers are defensible for some orders, which is exactly why grading them is hard.

So who checks the agent? 👀

Two thousand refunds a day. Nobody is sitting there reading two thousand refund letters, and nobody is going to be hired to do it either.

So we hand the job to another model, exactly the way we described at the start. We paste in the whole trace, we ask it to score the decision out of ten, and that number goes straight onto a slide.

pivot This is the turn of the talk. Slow right down here.

The claim

Your judge is
probably lying
to you.

Not on purpose, and not because anyone was careless. It hands you a confident number, in the right format, on time, and that number does not mean what you think it means.

thesis Let the slide sit for a beat before you talk over it.

How to think about it

A judge is an instrument.

Somewhere in your pipeline there is a thing that measures quality, and you have been reading whatever it prints as if it were a fact.

That makes it an instrument, and we have known for about four hundred years what you do with an instrument before you trust it. You calibrate it.

framing The instrument metaphor does a lot of work for the rest of the talk. Say it once and then stop explaining it.

Nobody would accept these

Three instruments you would throw out 🚫

🌡️ a thermometer

that reads differently every time you use it on the same thing

⚖️ a weighing scale

that quietly skips the heavy items and reports the average of the rest

📏 a ruler

that agrees with you mostly because everything you measure is the same length

All three are real failures in real judges, and you will see each one tonight, live in this room, with no terminal and no API key.

framing Each card maps to a clue: thermometer is clue one, scale is clue two, ruler is clue three. Clue four has no instrument, because it is not a measurement error. It is the wrong question.

Why I care about this one

When a model saw something that was not there 👀

I work on multimodal safety. My models look at a picture and decide whether to help.

One of them refused a request, and explained itself beautifully. It named the dangerous object in the image, told me where it was sitting, and declined.

There was no object. I had already removed it.

personal, part one Slow, first person, no hedging. Land the last line and then stop talking for a second.

What that taught me

It was refusing its own sentence.

The model was declining something that existed only inside the paragraph it had just written, and my evaluation scored that refusal as correct, because it read like a very good refusal.

My scoring never checked whether the model was right. It checked whether the model sounded right.

Every number I had published measured writing quality, and I was calling it safety. Since then I build the instrument before I trust the reading.

personal, part two Tie it straight back to the pizza: a judge that grades prose will love a confident wrong refund for exactly the same reason. That habit is what you are handing over tonight.

Clue one  //  the thermometer 🌡️

Same photo, different score.

I sent the same six hundred orders to the same judge, with the same prompt, three times. Temperature at zero. Nothing changed except the clock.

0% got the same score all three times
0% changed side of the pass line
0.0 pts average gap, highest to lowest pass
clue one Temperature zero is not determinism. Batching, routing and kernel nondeterminism all move the number. People assume this is solved. It is not. If you scored once, a quarter of your pass and fail labels were a coin toss.

Clue two  //  the scale that skips things ⚖️

The orders the judge never scored.

Twenty two percent came back blank, refused, or in a shape nothing could parse. Your pipeline drops those rows and reports the rest.

One percent of the clear photos went blank. Fifty seven percent of the blurry ones did.

clue two This is survivorship bias with a dropna in the middle of it. Ask who has written that line. Everyone has.

Clue two  //  the part that stings

It goes quiet on exactly the orders you needed it for.

The number you publish comes from the easy ones, because the hard ones never made it into the calculation at all.

Clue three  //  the ruler that always agrees 📏

Agreement that counts lucky guesses.

Seventy five percent agreement sounds like a working instrument. A judge that approves every order, reading nothing at all, scores fifty seven.

Take out the agreement you would get by luck and what is left is 0.50. Moderate, and moderate is not a gate.

clue three Do not say kappa first. Say the idea, then say the name once, then move on. Report the chance corrected number with the always approve baseline printed beside it, or the raw number flatters you every time.

Now put it back in the agent

Six steps, and the wobble multiplies.

One step held its verdict seventy three times in a hundred, which sounded survivable when I first saw it.

Across all six steps of one pizza refund, fifteen orders in a hundred come out the same way twice.

Nobody grades one step. We grade agents, and agents are made of steps.

compounding This is the slide the engineers photograph. Give it room and do not talk over the chart drawing in.

The number a team can act on

One version in four, named wrong.

Two versions of the refund agent. One of them really is better. You score a hundred orders on each and you ship the winner.

At a hundred orders you name the wrong version one time in four.

Nobody can act on a correlation. Everybody can act on that sentence, including the person who signs off your headcount.

decision risk Translate every eval metric into the decision it is used for. This is how you get budget for eval work.

Monday morning

Five things, one afternoon. ☕

  • Score everything three timesPublish the spread next to the average. If the spread embarrasses you, that is information.
  • Count what came back blankBreak the blanks down by how hard the case was. Never quietly drop a row.
  • Take out the luck before you celebratePrint the always approve baseline beside every agreement number you report.
  • Add a second judge that answers yes or noWas this the right call, not was this nicely written. Require both to pass.
  • Say what your eval size buys youTurn the metric into a sentence about how often you ship the wrong thing.
