Why I care about this

A model told me
what was in a photo.
It wasn't there.

I work on what models do when you give them an image. So I spend a lot of time watching them describe things confidently — and sometimes describe things that are not in the picture at all.

The part that kept me up: the evaluation gave that answer a good score. The model was wrong and the grader was happy.

That's when I stopped worrying about the model and started worrying about the grader.

Quick, for everyone

What is a judge, exactly?

the idea

You can't pay people to read a million answers. So you ask a model to score them.

why we all did it

It's cheap, it's instant, and it agrees with humans often enough to feel safe.

what it quietly became

A measuring instrument. One nobody ever asked for a spec sheet.

You wouldn't buy a kitchen scale that gives a different weight each time. We accept it from a judge because the answer comes back in good English.
8:30 – 9:45 Don't skip this even if the room looks technical. The kitchen scale line is the one people repeat afterwards, and it's what keeps the non-researchers with you.
"Instrument" is the word the rest of the talk runs on. Plant it here.

And it's worse for agents

One order isn't
one judgement

Read the photo. Check the order. Check the refund policy. Decide. Write the reply. Every step gets judged, and the errors don't cancel out.

9:45 – 10:45 Promise the number, don't give it yet: "in about four minutes I'll show you how fast that compounds, and it's worse than anyone guesses."
This is why the talk is about agents and not just chatbots.

Clue 01  //  what that means

27% of verdicts moved.
Now multiply that.

Six steps in one order, each judged. Judge the whole thing twice and ask how often you get the same answer.

Clue 02  //  what that means

It went quiet on
exactly the hard ones

On the photos anyone could read, it agrees with us. On the blurry ones it's guessing — and those are the ones it refused to answer more than half the time.

18:00 – 19:15 Say it plainly: the judge agrees with you about the orders you never needed help with.
Practical ask: split your results by something — photo quality, order size, how long the trace was. A single average hides this completely.

Clue 03

“It agrees with us
75% of the time”

Most refunds are easy. Both of us just say “fine”. And we count all of that as the judge being right.

A judge that approves everything scores 80% on a set that's 80% approvals. And knows nothing.
19:15 – 21:00 Shortest clue. The approve-everything judge is the whole argument.
One sentence of plain English for kappa: "it's agreement after you take away the credit for lucky guesses."
Ask: never put a raw agreement percentage in a report without the baseline next to it.

What it actually costs you

One call in four
is the wrong one

At 100 orders per version. You'd need 400 to get it right nine times out of ten.

Nobody can act on a correlation. Everybody can act on this.
24:45 – 26:15 The slide that changes what people do on Monday, because it's phrased as a shipping decision rather than a statistic.
Ask them to do the same sum for their own eval size.

Take this home

Five things,
none of them expensive

  • Run it three timesReport the middle score and the spread, not one number.
  • Count the blanks, then ask againPut them back in and publish the number you get after.
  • Split your results by difficultyOne average hides a judge that only works on easy cases.
  • Subtract the lucky guessesPrint the always-guess baseline next to your agreement number.
  • Ask a second judge a different question“Was it right?” — not just “was it good?”

Each one is one more API call and one more column in a spreadsheet.

Thank you

Judges aren't useless.
Unchecked ones are.

You can't make the judge honest. You can make it measurable — which is all we ask of anything else we measure with.
